Prologue

Night Seven

"You're trying to calculate us again, Nessa," said Kristian.

He was standing by the tall Venetian window, beyond which the night storm was hurling the black water of the lagoon against the marble balcony. He held a crystal glass of amber liquid, but he was not looking at the drink. Through his reflection in the glass his eyes were drilling into her.

"In that neat little restorer's head of yours everything must obey formulas," he continued, turning slowly. Each of his movements was calibrated with a surgeon's precision. "Beginning, climax, logical end. But you have forgotten the main rule of this palazzo: there is no logic here. There is only will."

Nessa sat in a deep velvet armchair at the centre of an enormous library drowning in half-dark.

"You are not will, Kristian," Nessa forced herself to lift her chin, even though her heart was hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird. "You are simply two sophisticated men who have mistaken themselves for gods in this rotting city. You think that because you bought those documents, you bought me?"

"We didn't buy you, little bird," came the low, hoarse voice of Damiano from the shadow by the bookshelves.

He moved into the light slowly, with the predatory grace of a wild animal. Heavy footfalls on the dark parquet.

His black shirt open at the collar, fabric stretched across broad shoulders, the gaze of dark eyes — direct, consuming, leaving no room to manoeuvre.

Damiano stopped directly in front of her armchair, leaned down, pressing his palms into the armrests, caging Nessa between his body and the chair. He smelled of rain and danger.

"If we had wanted to buy you," Damiano growled, bending close to her ear so that his hot breath scorched the sensitive skin of her neck, "you would already be sitting in chains on one of the boats heading east. But you're sitting in our chair. Drinking our wine. And trying to prick us with your childish little barbs."

"Because you are keeping me here!" Nessa tried to push him away from his chest, but his body felt like a granite wall. Her fingers only gripped the expensive fabric of his shirt uselessly. "Both of you! You play the saviours, but in reality you—"

"In reality we what?" Kristian cut in softly, moving up behind her. His cool palm settled on the back of her head, fingers tangling in her dark hair, gently but unmistakably holding her head, forcing her to look directly into Damiano's eyes. "Finish the sentence, restorer. What are we? Monsters? Or the only people in all of Venice who have seen that beneath your demure skirt and correct manners a wild, hungry creature is hiding — one that craves destruction?"

"I'm not like that…" she breathed, and that breath escaped as a faltering, treacherous whisper.

"You are," Damiano said quietly, the word cut clean. His large, rough palm slid slowly along her knee and upward, just above the hem of her silk dress, leaving a trail of fire and goosebumps. "You're not trembling from fear, Nessa. You're trembling because you madly love being held by two. You're a forgery. Just as beautiful and dangerous a forgery as the paintings Kristian burns in the fireplace."

"Be quiet…" Nessa squeezed her eyes shut, trying to escape their presence, this suffocating, disorienting combination of Kristian's cold aestheticism and Damiano's wild, animal force.

"Don't close your eyes," Damiano ordered, tightening his fingers slightly in her hair. His voice was quiet, but it carried a steel that brooked no argument. "Look at him. Look at me. You wanted to unravel our secret, restorer? You have it. Now pay the price."

"What price?" Her voice broke.

Damiano smiled — dark, reckless — his lips barely grazing her trembling lower lip without kissing it, holding the agonising, maddening pause.

"Our price, Nessa. You stay with us. Not as a prisoner. But as our chief sinner."

Chapter 1

Restorer of Dead Texts

POV: Nessa Vane

Nessa Vane knew how to bring the dead back to life.

Not, of course, those lying in oak coffins beneath frozen earth, but those woven from compressed linen, cloth rags, dry ink and leather bindings.

For her there was no difference: an ancient manuscript or a time-rotted folio — every object that came into her workshop had its own pulse, its own medical history, its own scars.

She had turned twenty-four only a couple of months ago. The age at which most of her contemporaries were building careers in glossy publishing houses, posting aesthetic photographs from trendy cafes and looking for the ideal life partner.

Nessa spent fourteen hours a day in a semi-basement space on the edge of an old quarter, where her constant companions were the smell of tannic acid, beeswax, and the persistent thin sound of rain against the blind windows.

From the outside Nessa seemed fragile — almost weightless. Tall, fine-boned, with pale, almost porcelain skin on which her collarbones showed sharply. Her dark, nearly black hair was usually pinned up carelessly with a long wooden paper-spike, so that several soft strands eternally escaped and fell across her slender neck.

There was no glossy, polished beauty about her — Nessa's attractiveness bloomed in half-dark, when she stopped hunching and shed her habitual guardedness.

But the main feature of her face was her eyes. A deep, rare green — like dark nephrite or a reed-choked lake pool. An attentive, extraordinarily sharp, "reading" gaze.

She looked at the world as though trying to make out a hidden text or an ancient watermark on the forehead of every person she spoke to.

"You're sitting without light again, Nessa," came a creaking, familiar voice from the doorway.

She did not even raise her head from the worktable.

"The magnifying lamp gives enough light, Marco. Besides, daylight is destructive to seventeenth-century parchment. You know that."

Old Marco — the owner of the bakery on the floor above, the only person who ever dropped in just to visit — sighed and set a paper bag on the edge of her workbench. From it came the divine scent of fresh cinnamon and hot baking.

"You'll ruin your eyes, girl. And your back. At twenty-four you should be walking along the embankment, going on dates — not pulling rot out of old folios."

"It isn't rot," Nessa objected quietly but with surprising firmness. Her slender fingers in nitrile gloves paused above a book's spine as usual. "It's patina. The trace of time."

"The trace of time they pay you pennies for," Marco shook his head, looking around the workshop.

The space did indeed resemble an alchemist's den. Along the walls rose oak shelves crowded with jars of diluted wheat starch, bone glue, alcohol solutions, oak-gall tinctures, and small boxes of gold leaf.

On a special drying press lay the cleaned pages of a psalter, and on the walls hung antique engravings with anatomical cross-sections of book bindings.

This was her home. And her refuge.

After her parents were killed on an icy road when Nessa was barely twenty, she was left absolutely alone. Without connections, without patrons, with an enormous debt on the family flat.

She had to sell everything, abandon a prestigious faculty of art history and descend into the lowlands of the craft — to where what was required was not social small talk but hellish, painstaking patience.

She learned to be invisible. She pulled on close-necked, covering sweaters, stopped using cosmetics, and buried her sensitivity deep beneath a layer of restraint and impeccable manners. Outwardly she seemed a modest, quiet restorer — the perfect grey mouse.

But inside Nessa lived an entirely different person.

Beneath that restraint burned a dangerous, almost mad hunger. Nessa was greedy. Not for money — money interested her only as a means of paying rent on this workshop.

She was greedy for secrets, for rare texts, for stories no one was supposed to read. Within her lived a suppressed, deep sensuality that she was afraid of herself.

She loved the sensation of control over old manuscripts, loved the risk when a single wrong press of the scalpel could destroy a unique document worth tens of thousands of dollars.

"Thank you for the roll, Marco," she said, softening slightly, smiling for the first time that day.

The smile instantly changed her face, and a warm, living light flared in her green eyes.

"I'll eat. Honestly. As soon as I finish this fold."

The old man shook his head but chose not to argue. He patted her on the shoulder and left, pulling the heavy oak door shut quietly behind him.

Silence fell again.

Nessa took a slow breath, savouring the familiar symphony of smells: dry paper, beeswax, alcohol and sweet cinnamon. She picked up the bone smoother and went back to work.

There was no room for surprises in her life. Every morning began with a cup of black coffee without sugar, every evening ended with a walk to the letterbox.

In this routine lay her safety. No one could hurt her, no one could betray her or make her feel helpless, as long as she was surrounded by her silent, loyal books.

She did not suspect that her safe world would collapse in exactly twenty minutes.

At around six in the evening, when it had grown fully dark outside and the rain had turned into a monotonous icy drizzle, there was a knock at the door.

The sound was strange. Not Marco's soft knock, not a courier's impatient rapping. This was a single, heavy, metallic blow that made the small glass jars on the top shelf ring.

Nessa flinched. The scalpel in her hand froze above the page. Her green eyes narrowed.

"It's locked!" she called out loudly, rising from her high stool. "The workshop is closed to visitors!"

No answer came. Beyond the door there were no footsteps, no breathing. Only the monotonous sound of water running down the drainpipe.

Nessa waited a minute. For some reason her heart gave a heavy, uneasy thud. She went to the door, clicked the bolt and carefully pulled the handle towards her.

There was no one on the threshold. The street was empty, flooded with grey puddles in which a few dull streetlamps were reflected.

But directly on the icy stone threshold lay an object.

Nessa frowned and bent down. It was an envelope. But not of ordinary postal kraft paper. It had been cut from dense, extraordinarily expensive card of a charcoal-black colour with a rough texture that called to mind fine silk. No stamp, no postcode.

At the centre of the rectangle sat a massive seal of sealing wax — a deep, midnight colour shot through with golden mica.

The impression in the wax showed clearly: a snake coiled into a perfect figure-of-eight, swallowing its own tail, while within its rings two carved gondola oars crossed.

On the front of the envelope, in bronze ink — an impeccable, calligraphic hand — was written:

"Nessa Vane. To be delivered by hand."

Nessa picked up the envelope. It was cold and settled heavily in her palm. The sealing wax held a scent alien to her workshop: bitter almond and spicy, stifling frankincense.

She returned to her worktable, bolted the door, and took up the bone knife. Her fingers trembled slightly — not from fear, but from that damned hunger that had flared within her.

She slit the edge of the envelope.

Inside were two items.

The first — a card of black paper with a gold border:

"Night Seven. Palazzo Morosini-Gibilterra, Venice. Arrival strictly before 23:00 by maritime time. Mask obligatory. Possession of this invitation confirms your status and right to the unconditional stake."

But the second item was an insert.

Nessa pulled it out, and her knees weakened. She dropped heavily onto her stool, her green eyes wide with astonishment, forgetting Marco and his rolls and the entire rest of the world.

It was a fragment of folded seventeenth-century parchment — the thinnest, milky-white vellum, made from skin. The workmanship of the masters of the legendary Venetian printing house. On the parchment, in a close Latin script interspersed with mathematical formulae, was a text.

Before her lay an original page from the "Cipher of Manutius" — the considered-destroyed catalogue of the closed archive of the Venetian Republic. The dream of every archivist on the planet.

In the margin of the parchment, in the same bronze ink, a fresh annotation had been made:

"The complete folio will be put up for auction at the first midnight. If you want history to survive, Vane, come and take it. Or watch it bought by those who will lock it in a safe for ever."

Nessa sat in complete silence, staring at the black sealing wax.

Her mind was screaming: "This is a trap! How do they know your name? How do they know about the Cipher?"

But her heart — her hidden, hungry nature — had already made its decision. Her green eyes darkened, taking on the colour of storm water.

She would look this devil in the face. She would go to Venice.
