Prologue

Eight years ago…

POV: Adrian Vincenzo

In our business only those survive who know how to count seconds, read people before they reach for the weapon under their jacket, and shut down their own emotions without mercy.

The rainy half-dark beyond the panoramic windows was giving way, grudgingly, to the heavy golden light of a setting sun. At this hour the Vesper casino was suspended in that brittle, almost reverent silence that comes only before a storm.

I stood in the shadow of the second-floor gallery — an establishment that Vittorio Romano had entrusted to my full control two years before. From here the entire glass-walled hall, the empty gaming tables and the burgundy carpet lay spread out before me like an open palm.

Every detail of this place had been calibrated by me: from the evacuation routes and sniper sightlines down to the thickness of the velvet drapes.

Behind me already lay years of the Syndicate's ruthless, merciless schooling. From the ragged, hungry boy Vittorio had pulled out of the poverty and scorching dust of Siracusa, I had become a man whose name in Liguria made even the boldest capos lower their voices.

I was the manager — his finest pupil and right hand. At twenty-four I had learned to be a cold, flawless mechanism. I genuinely believed that Vittorio had long since burned out everything superfluous in me, leaving only dry calculation and absolute loyalty.

I was holding a heavy leather folder of documents from a Swiss bank. I needed to hand them to Vittorio. It was time to go down.

But I lingered. For one simple and very dangerous reason.

Down below, moving slowly between the gaming tables, was she.

Fifteen-year-old Isabella.

She had absolutely no business being here — among this temple of sin and vice, amid the air thick with wax on antique furniture, fine leather, Cuban tobacco and the heavy, suffocating cologne of her grandfather. A girl from an entirely different world, whom Vittorio had for the first time decided to bring along to "see the real underbelly of the family business."

She was trailing the tips of her fingers along the polished wood rim of the roulette wheel, studying the perfect sectors of the disc with quiet fascination. I watched her every move from above. In her bearing — that childlike yet already so thoroughbred grace — there was something that made my fingers tighten on the folder.

When Vittorio laughed softly and drew his granddaughter close to his shoulder, I stepped out of the gallery's shadow.

"Vittorio, the documents from the Swiss bank have arrived. They're waiting for your signature."

My voice came out exactly as it should have: deep, level, with the icy metallic hardness I had hammered into it over years.

I began descending the wide curved staircase at an unhurried pace. I wore a black silk shirt with the top buttons carelessly undone and sleeves rolled to the elbows. On my left cheekbone a thin, neat scar still ached faintly from a fresh wound — a vivid reminder of what power costs in Liguria and how much blood it takes to hold this hall.

She turned. Isabella held her breath, and I registered that microscopic stumble in her chest with absolute clarity.

My gaze swept the room and settled on her. I looked at her the way I always did — cold, scanning, the way one assesses a potential threat or a high-risk asset. But for the first time in many years my icy, honed control developed a perceptible crack.

My pulse fired two ragged, heavy beats that reverberated somewhere in my throat.

"Elevated threat in sector" — my mind constructed the thought instantly. I grabbed at that dry, safe formulation by habit. Assessment of risk to the security of the Syndicate head's granddaughter. Nothing more. This was simply professional reflex.

"Adrian," Vittorio nodded in greeting, and I noticed again the respect with which he addressed me in her presence. "Come closer. I want you to finally meet my granddaughter. Isabelle, this is Adrian. The man who understands this business better than anyone else. My finest pupil and my right hand."

I closed the distance between us without a sound, stopping a couple of paces away. The air between us seemed to thicken and pull taut like a bowstring. She smelled of something clean, young and unreachable — a startling contrast to the smell of gunpowder, bitter coffee and rain I was accustomed to.

"A pleasure, Isabella," I said, dipping my head slightly.

She lifted her chin stubbornly, forcing herself not to look away from my eyes. Fifteen years old and already pride, wildness and inner fire enough for an entire army.

"Grandfather says you control the rules."

An almost imperceptible, ghostly smile touched my lips against my will. I dropped my gaze to her slender fingers still resting on the roulette cloth, then looked back directly into her face. Inside, somewhere under my ribs, a strange, alien, dangerous impulse flared. The desire to shield her from this world. The desire to break that pride of hers. The desire to place myself between her and every piece of filth in the Syndicate so that none of them would dare let their eyes touch her.

"Rules exist to make guests feel safe, Princess," I said quietly, taking an almost imperceptible step forward, filling her space with my presence and catching her instant confusion. "But real power begins where rules end."

"Adrian is right," Vittorio interjected gently, watching our exchange of glances with barely concealed interest. "But Isabelle finds it too quiet here during the day. She thinks Vesper is asleep."

I shook my head slowly, never taking my piercing gaze from Isabella. I saw her breathing quicken, a faint flush rise on her cheeks, the tremor she was trying to hide behind a stubborn mask.

"Silence is deceptive, Isabella," my voice dropped to a half-whisper, vibrating in the empty hall. "This place never sleeps. It simply waits for its hour. And… some places choose people long before people choose them."

I turned and moved towards Vittorio's office with a light, predatory stride. My back burned. I felt her gaze between my shoulder blades as physically as a touch.

I told myself it was merely professional analysis. That I was concerned only with the security of the Syndicate's heir. But standing there, I did not yet know how much dust and blood we would swallow together, how many times I would have to shield her with my body from bursts of fire in Ligurian alleys, or how deeply that unconscious, dull, poisoning pull would grow into my bones and bring me to my knees.

All of it had begun long before that night. Long before Vesper rose above us.

It had all begun in the scorching filth of Siracusa, where a boy named Adrian had nothing but a shard of steel in his pocket and an animal hunger to survive…

Chapter 1

Streets of Siracusa

POV: Adrian Vincenzo

No one is born with ice-cold steel in place of a heart. It is forged. Scar by scar, betrayal by betrayal, fire by fire — until nothing remains inside but a dry, razor hunger to survive.

In Siracusa the sun does not warm. It burns.

It fuses itself into the scorching, salt-eaten cobblestones, works its way under your nails together with the Sicilian dust, and makes the air above the harbour melt into a liquid, suffocating haze.

At twelve I knew everything about this city that a person needs to know in order not to die before sunset.

I knew which alleys in the Ortigia quarter turned into dead-end traps at high tide, which traders hid rotten fish behind the back door and which kept a loaded shotgun there, and how many seconds it took a man to bleed out when a shard of steel entered cleanly below the left collarbone.

The street teaches a simple and merciless anatomy: if you run slowly, you starve. If you trust people, your throat is cut in the first doorway for a sail of sacking and a pair of worn-out boots. If you cannot read other people's shadows, you quickly become someone else's prey.

But I was not always a beast. There was a time when I had something else, and my fingers did not close on a blade at every sound.

* * *

I remembered a time when I had a home. Small, slightly stooped, with walls of whitewashed stone, smelling of oregano, fresh olive oil and sea salt soaked into the wooden beams.

It stood on the very edge of Siracusa, where the dry hills broke straight down into the turquoise sea.

I remembered my father — a tacit, work-bent fisherman with black, calloused hands.

When the sea was calm he would carry me on his shoulders, laugh his hoarse, rolling laugh and teach me to tie sailors' knots.

"A knot must be stronger than a man's word, Adrian," he would tell me, pulling at the rough hemp rope. "A word can be taken back. A good knot — never."

And my mother… Her face had already blurred in my memory into a hazy silhouette, but I would carry her dark, perpetually wind-tangled hair and her smell to the end of my life.

She always smelled of cheap citrus soap and baked bread.

When she stroked my head in the evenings, I felt that this house was the safest place on earth.

We were never wealthy. We ate cheap anchovies and washed them down with wine diluted with water.

But we were alive. Until the cursed night when someone knocked at our door.

In Siracusa small traders and harbour fishermen always pay for "protection" from the local muscle. These are not the rules of the law; they are the law of survival.

My father paid faithfully — every month he handed over his last money, handed over his best catch, cut the best portion from our already modest table, all so those shadows in dirty caps would not appear on our doorstep.

But that year September was cruel. The sea stormed for three weeks running. Black, foaming waves smashed the piers, nets tore on underwater reefs, and there was no fish at all.

My father came back from the shore empty-handed, with bloodied, battered hands and dark, deadened eyes. His debt to the quarter gang grew day by day like a tumour.

They came at midnight.

I was only eight years old. I slept on a narrow wooden bench in the corner of the single room, covered by an old wool blanket.

The door did not simply open — it was kicked in with a heavy boot, so the pine hinges cracked and flew out of the frame.

Three men burst into the room. They reeked of cheap drink, rotten teeth and soot.

I remember that entire night not as a connected memory but as flashes of white-hot iron seared into my brain:

My mother's piercing scream splitting the dark.

The heavy, dull, hollow sound of my father crashing to the stone floor after a blow from an iron poker across his head.

The clatter of smashing clay crockery.

And fire.

They had not come merely for money — we had none in any case. They had come to teach a lesson to the entire coastline. So that every fisherman would know what happened to those who failed to fill the local masters' pockets on time.

One of them threw a burning torch directly onto the dry wooden beams of the ceiling and onto the pile of dry nets stacked by the door. The dry pine caught instantly, turning the small room into a blazing hell.

They wedged the kicked-in door shut from the outside with a heavy beam, laughing and swearing in the local dialect.

The room filled with black, acrid smoke. I coughed, choking, tears burning my eyes.

My father, covered in blood, his face split from brow to chin, by some superhuman effort of will dragged himself to me. With his burning, bloodied hands he grabbed me by the scruff of the neck and hauled me to the far corner of the kitchen.

There, behind an old flour chest, was a small ventilation opening — a narrow square shaft for air circulation, too tight for a grown man to squeeze through but passable for a child.

"Run, Adrian!" my father rasped, smashing out the wooden grating with a single blow. His fingers were charred, foam at his mouth. "Run and don't look back! Don't come back!"

He pushed me outside, into the damp, cold dark and dirt of the back yard. I fell into thorny blackberry bushes, tearing my face and hands.

Behind me came a terrible, deafening crack — the burning roof of our house collapsed inward with a grinding roar and a sea of sparks, burying my parents and everything I had ever loved.

I did not cry out. I did not weep.

That night something in me, an eight-year-old boy, burned out and died together with that fire. In the mud behind the blazing house, an entirely different person was born.

* * *

So I ended up on the street.

Without family. Without shelter.

Without a name, without a single dry crust of bread in my pocket.

The first six months were an unbroken, hellish circle of survival.

For tourists from Rome or Milan, Siracusa looked like paradise — white-stone squares, magnificent ancient ruins, a blue sea and the aroma of freshly brewed espresso from street cafes. For a homeless orphan this city turned into harsh, merciless jungle where every day one had to prove one's right to breathe.

I was beaten. Beaten regularly, brutally, and for nothing.

Older street boys knocked out of me the last coins I managed to beg on the square by the cathedral. Harbour dogs drove me from the cellars where I tried to shelter from the heavy autumn rains. Traders shooed me away with bamboo canes the moment I approached their stalls.

I slept in rotting wooden crates that had held citrus fruit, on damp stones at the shore, in abandoned catacombs beneath the city that smelled of damp and death.

I ate whatever I found in the rot behind the fish market: discarded tuna heads, vegetable trimmings, mouldy crusts of bread.

But I was learning. Learning with the hunger of a wild animal.

The street taught me its main lessons.

I understood: tears do not stop a raised fist. Pleas for mercy produce in the strong only a contemptuous smirk and the desire to hit harder.

The only thing respected in this filth, the only language these creatures understand, is strength, the absence of fear, and an absolute readiness to kill first.

I forced myself to forget everything. Forgot the smell of my mother's citrus soap. Forgot the warmth of my father's hands. Forgot the taste of normal food. I burned out of myself every trace of weakness.

By twelve, nothing remained of that frightened, weeping boy from the burning house — not even a shadow. My body had lengthened and grown sinewy as a dry grapevine. My cheeks had hollowed, my skin darkened under the constant Sicilian sun and become covered in small scars.

But the main change was in my eyes. In them there was no longer any childlike curiosity. Only a dry, icy, razor-sharp calculation.

I had memorised Siracusa by heart. I knew every doorway, every cornice to drop from, every crack in the ancient stone walls.

I had learned to move in absolute silence, to read people's intentions from barely perceptible shifts of the shoulder or changes in a glance, and to sleep with open eyes, alert to the slightest sound.

I found myself a weapon.

In the ruins of an old forge I dug up a strip of steel. For three weeks — day after day, hour after hour — I sharpened it on the granite steps of the cathedral until it became a narrow, heavy stiletto with a point capable of piercing thick leather.

This was not a weapon of pride. It was the only argument that had kept me alive.

* * *

That day the sun stood at its zenith, turning Siracusa into a blazing furnace.

I sat on the edge of a stone parapet by the Ortigia harbour market, knees drawn to my chest. In the shade it was barely cooler than in the sun, but from here the view was perfect, absolute.

Behind me — a narrow alley leading into the residential quarters. To my left — a descent to the sea. To my right — a stack of empty fishing barrels.

Three escape routes. Not one blind spot. Every detail of the space processed automatically by my mind, without wasted thought.

In my right hand, hidden in the deep fold of my dirty-grey, hand-me-down trousers, my blade rested in its usual place. My fingers closed around the cool, cord-wrapped metal.

"Hey, orphan!" called Mario, a fat, red-faced tuna trader, wiping his sweating bull-neck with a dirty apron. "Get away from the stall! You're scaring off my customers with that dog look of yours."

I did not stir. Did not straighten, did not look away. I simply shifted my eyes to him, slowly, very smoothly.

Mario stumbled on my gaze. He opened his mouth to deliver another round of abuse, but the words stuck in his throat.

At twelve, my gaze made grown men uncomfortable. There was no anger in it. There was a dry, mathematical assessment.

I scanned him: weight class — over a hundred kilos, but heavy wheezing, weak knees, left leg with a limp. In his right hand — a heavy fish-gutting knife, but his grip was poor, his fingers slippery with fat. If he took one step towards me, I could drop from the parapet, drive my blade under his kneecap, and dissolve into the alley four seconds before his thickheaded apprentice came running from the cellar.

The trader muttered under his breath, spat nervously on the scorching stones and quickly turned away, making a show of being entirely absorbed in butchering a tuna carcass.

Weak. In Siracusa the weak do not last long. They merely create noise.

But that midday the familiar, monotonous rhythm of the harbour suddenly broke.

It was not an audible sound — it was instinct, sharpened by years of poverty and danger. The air seemed to thicken.

The street pickpockets who usually threaded through the buyers instantly vanished into dark doorways. Traders fell silent, hawkers shut their mouths, and harbour porters straightened their stooped backs, murmuring to one another.

Towards the far pier — where only old, peeling fishing trawlers usually moored — a yacht was moving slowly in.

It looked like a foreign monster against this impoverished shore. Enormous, with a dark, almost blue-black lacquered hull, tinted panoramic glass. It radiated an alien, incomprehensible wealth and that particular, coiled menace that cannot be mistaken for anything else.

This was not the power of a street gang with knuckledusters. This was power that moves governments.

The gangplank lowered with a quiet hydraulic hiss.

Four men emerged. Three — tall, lean, in identical dark-grey suits despite the savage Sicilian heat. Their movements were fluid, economical, almost synchronised. Their right hands rested unobtrusively under their jacket lapels, while their eyes ceaselessly scanned the roofs of nearby buildings, windows and the crowd.

True professionals. Killers who had been through a hard school.

And in the centre walked he.

A man in an impeccable grey three-piece suit of the finest wool. Hair slicked back perfectly, a heavy cane with a carved silver handle in his right hand. He appeared to be around fifty, but in his bearing, in the set of his broad shoulders, there was a monolithic, unbreakable power.

He did not shout, did not reach for a weapon, did not look about him uneasily. He had no need to. His mere presence made the hot, stifling Siracusa air feel icy. The whole square froze, submitting to his silent authority.

Vittorio Romano.

Head of shadow Liguria. The man whose name in certain circles was spoken only in whispers.

He walked along the fish stalls with the unhurried grace of a man strolling through his own country estate.

And then one of the local fools — a seventeen-year-old thief named Cesare, desperate and stupid — decided this was his luckiest day.

Cesare burst from his hiding place behind a spreading olive tree, intending to slice the fat leather wallet from the last bodyguard's belt and bolt for the catacombs.

Everything happened instantly. In a second and a half.

The guard didn't even reach for the pistol inside his jacket. A short, barely visible movement — a precise, terrible elbow strike to the throat. A dry crack. Cesare dropped in a heap onto the dusty, scorching stones, writhing, coughing blood, gasping for air. The second bodyguard clicked his safety with a dry, almost soundless motion, levelling a black muzzle directly at the head of the man on the ground.

The market crowd gasped and lurched back in terror, crushing one another. Traders dived under their counters.

I stayed sitting on the parapet.

I did not run. I didn't even flinch.

My mind registered the details coldly, by habit: viewing angle of the bodyguard with the pistol — forty-five degrees. The second guard covering the approach on the left. The third controlling the roof of the market awning. The man with the cane… the man with the cane had not moved a muscle.

I was not looking at the steel of the pistol. I was looking directly into the face of the man in the grey suit.

Vittorio stopped. On his aristocratic face, deeply lined, not a trace of anger, irritation or surprise.

He looked at the whimpering, choking Cesare the way one looks at a beetle accidentally crushed underfoot on the road.

And then… he slowly moved his head to one side.

And his gaze drove straight into me.

Amid the panicking, surging crowd, amid the fear and the cries, I was the single static object in the entire square.

A boy with sunken, hollow cheeks, in dirty rags, gripping a piece of sharpened steel in his pocket. A boy who had not looked away.

We looked at each other for a second, two, three.

Time seemed not to exist.

I looked directly into his steel, piercing eyes. Without fear. Without pleading. With the icy, feral detachment that comes only from an absolute absence of hope and a readiness to die at any moment. I expected no mercy. I was only calculating how many of his men I could take with me if they decided to clear the square.

Vittorio slowly raised his left hand in an expensive leather glove, stopping the bodyguard whose finger was already tightening on the trigger.

He took a step towards me. The heavy, measured thud of his wooden cane on the stone pavement rang out through the empty harbour like blows on a bell.

He came almost close enough to touch. He smelled of expensive Cuban tobacco, a perfume unknown to me, and… absolute, concentrated power.

A startling contrast to the rotten fish and city drains I was used to.

"What is your name, boy?" His voice was hoarse, deep, rising from his chest. He did not shout, but that voice silenced even the gulls over the sea.

"Adrian," I answered, the word clipped.

My voice did not waver. Did not crack.

Even though my heart under my ribs was hammering a wild, ragged, staccato beat, and my fingers in my pocket had tightened on the blade until the skin on my knuckles went white.

Vittorio dropped his heavy gaze to my right hand, motionless in the deep pocket of my trousers.

A barely perceptible, hard, appraising smile appeared on his thin lips. A man of his level had understood instantly what I was holding there.

"Do you know who I am, Adrian?" he asked quietly, tilting his head slightly.

"I don't care who you are," I cut back, leaning forward a fraction and shifting my weight to the balls of my feet, ready to spring. "But if your man takes one more step towards me, I'll put this in his artery before he can pull the trigger."

The bodyguards tensed instantly, fingers going to triggers, the atmosphere drawn tight to its limit. One movement and I would have been turned into a sieve.

But Vittorio simply laughed, quietly and briefly.

That laugh sounded like dry ice cracking. There was no mockery in it — only deep, professional satisfaction. The way a collector contemplates an extraordinarily rare, lethal artefact discovered by chance in a pile of street rubbish.

"No panic. No fear. No childish blubbering," said Romano, studying my pupils as though reading my entire short, broken life. "Just cold calculation, hatred and an animal instinct to survive."

He took half a step back and rested both hands on the top of his cane.

"Siracusa will rot with you, boy. Here you will either die from a knife in a doorway within a couple of months, or you'll become the same blunt meat as that wretch on the stones. But I have a place where such steel is smelted into a real blade."

Vittorio turned and walked away unhurriedly towards a convoy of three black armoured saloons halted at the entrance to the pier.

At the door of the lead car Romano stopped. He did not turn around. He only dropped a short phrase over his shoulder that cut my life into before and after:

"Come, Adrian. Or stay and rot in this dust."

I looked at the whimpering Cesare lying in his own blood, to whom nobody had gone.

I looked at my own dirty, bloodied fingers.

I looked at the scorching, impoverished streets of Siracusa that had taken my father, my mother, and my childhood.

And then I took my sharpened piece of steel from my pocket and slowly opened my fingers. The shard rang once on the hot granite and lay still.

I no longer needed it.

I rose from the parapet and took my first step after the man who had pulled me from hell to forge me into something far more dangerous.

That day I left Siracusa behind me for ever.
